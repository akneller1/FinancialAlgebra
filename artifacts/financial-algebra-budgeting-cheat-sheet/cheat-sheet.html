Financial Algebra · Unit 2: Budgeting & Spending

Essential vocabulary

Budget
A plan that assigns expected income to spending, saving, and debt payments for a period of time
Cash flow
Money coming in minus money going out during a period
Surplus
The amount left over when income is greater than expenses
Deficit
The shortfall when expenses are greater than income
Net income (take-home pay)
Pay remaining after taxes and other deductions; the amount a budget should be built on
Sinking fund
Money set aside each month for a known, irregular future expense
Fixed expense
A cost that stays the same amount each period
Variable expense
A cost whose amount changes from period to period
Discretionary expense
Spending on wants that could be reduced or eliminated without affecting basic needs
Essential expense (need)
Spending required for basic living or to keep earning income
50/30/20 guideline
A starting-point plan that directs about 50% of take-home pay to needs, 30% to wants, and 20% to savings and extra debt payments
Emergency fund
Savings reserved for unexpected expenses or income loss, often targeted at 3–6 months of essential expenses
Financial constraint
A limit, such as income or a savings target, that restricts which spending choices are possible
Savings goal
A specific amount of money to accumulate by a target date
Opportunity cost
The value of the next-best alternative given up when making a choice
Unit price
The cost per single unit (ounce, item, load) used to compare package sizes fairly
Impulse purchase
An unplanned purchase made on the spot, often influenced by marketing or emotion
Buy now, pay later (BNPL)
A short-term financing plan that splits a purchase into installments, sometimes with late fees or interest
Sales tax
A percent of the purchase price collected by the state at the time of sale

Common misconceptions

  • Budgeting with gross pay instead of take-home pay.
  • “Biweekly” ≠ “twice a month” (26 vs 24 checks).
  • A sale is savings only if you planned to buy the item.
  • A lower monthly payment does not mean a lower total cost.

Worked example

  1. Biweekly take-home $1,300 → 1,300 × 2612 = $2,816.67/month
  2. Expenses $2,640 → cash flow +$176.67
  3. Car insurance $780 every 6 months → set aside $130/month
  4. True cash flow = 176.67 − 130 = +$46.67 (thin margin!)

Questions to ask before deciding

  • Is this a need or a want — and who decides?
  • What will this cost per year?
  • What am I giving up (opportunity cost)?
  • Which irregular bills are coming in the next 12 months?
  • What will I do if income drops for a month?

Decision tips

  • Pay yourself first: automate savings on payday.
  • Round hours and months UP; round maximum spending DOWN.
  • Review subscriptions every 3 months.
  • Keep a starter emergency fund before extra spending.
